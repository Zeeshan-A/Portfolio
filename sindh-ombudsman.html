Case study · P. 01

GovTechSindh Government

Sindh Ombudsman: a 30-year paper process, moved online.

Citizens filed complaints in person, and the average case took 120 days to close. I designed the system that replaced the paper files: citizen apps on web, mobile and tablet, and the tools staff and officers use to run each case. In its first 9 months it handled 18,000+ complaints, against 7–8K a year on paper.

01 Context

What is the Sindh Ombudsman?

The Sindh Ombudsman's office investigates citizens' complaints against provincial government departments. For 30 years, every complaint was filed, tracked and resolved on paper, with case files moving by hand between offices across the province.

Three groups depend on the same case file.

RoleWhat they needWhat paper made hard
CitizensFile a complaint and follow it to a decisionFiling meant a visit; after that, no way to check progress
Regional office staffRegister, find and update complaintsSearching handwritten registers by name or date
Case officersRun hearings, decide and close casesCause lists and disposals kept by hand, with no live view of what was pending

My part

I was the only designer, working with a PM, a business analyst and the developers. I owned the information architecture: I mapped the paper process into 10+ modules and the navigation between them. I designed every citizen and staff screen across web, mobile and tablet, built the component system, handed off to the developers and ran design QA. The product owner ran the on-site sessions with staff, mainly in Sukkur, and I joined remotely.

02 Problem

A complaint took 120 days on average, and nobody could see where it was.

Citizens had to file in person on a long paper form, then wait with no way to check progress. Staff couldn't answer "where is my case?" without pulling physical files, and officers had no live view of what was pending or overdue. The office needed a system citizens could file in and follow, and staff could run cases in, without losing the steps set out in the office's manuals.

02 A

Filing took a visit and a long form

The paper form asked for everything at once, in a single office visit.

02 B

After filing, silence

Citizens heard nothing until a letter or hearing notice arrived. To check progress they called or visited, and staff had to find the file to answer.

02 C

Finding a case meant searching by hand

Registers were handwritten and searched by name or date, so every hearing and reply started with a hunt for the folder.

02 D

No live view for officers

Cause lists and disposals were written by hand, so pending, overdue and escalated cases only showed up when someone counted them.

03 Investigation

Learning the paper process before designing a screen.

The project ran inside the Ombudsman's office, so citizens weren't part of the research. We started with the people who handle every complaint.

The product owner ran sessions on site with 8+ staff and officers, mainly in Sukkur, and I joined remotely. Staff sent case files, registers and complaint material through shared channels and Teams meetings.

MethodWith whomWhat it gave us

What we found became the four problems above. The order a case moves through the office's manuals became the structure of the case page.

04 Design decisions

Each decision answers one of the four problems.

The PM set the order of the modules. For each one, I designed low-fidelity wireframes and a clickable prototype for the team to review, then high-fidelity screens and a prototype that we presented to the Sindh Ombudsman's team for approval.

From problem to design decision

ProblemDesign decision
02 A Long, one-shot registrationStepped registration: one short step at a time, with progress shown
02 B No visibility after filingLifecycle stepper and status badges on every complaint
02 C Slow case lookupSearch by CNIC or complaint number, from any screen
02 D Manual hearings and disposalsHearing/Cause List module and bulk case disposal

The citizen's happy path

Every screen was drawn in greyscale first (22 wireframes), then taken to high fidelity. These are 17 of the key screens citizens and staff use today.

H.01

Stepped registration

Answers 02 A

Instead of one long form, registration is three short screens: log in or register, choose your citizen type, then fill in only what's required. Pakistani citizens, overseas Pakistanis and foreigners need different details, so the type comes first and the form adapts. Optional details stay folded, so first-time filers aren't faced with every field at once. Urdu is one tap away from the first screen. Estimated time to file: about 3 minutes.

H.02

One complaint, one reference, three tabs

Answers 02 B

Every complaint gets a reference like KHI-AK-EDU-0001/2025, built from region, department and year, so anyone can tell where it belongs from the code alone. The Activity tab is a numbered timeline of who acted, when and with which documents. That's the answer to "where is my case?" that used to need a phone call. When a case closes, the citizen can rate the decision.

H.03

Search and insights for staff

Answers 02 C and 02 D

Any case can be found by CNIC or complaint number from any screen; paper registers could only be searched by name or date. The dashboard is the first screen officers see, and it changes by role: each role sees only the numbers for its own work. Overdue and escalated counts sit beside the totals, and district charts show which offices are falling behind. The whole dashboard mirrors into Urdu right to left, not just the labels.

H.04

Nine stages, one case page

Answers 02 D

The paper file became one page with nine tabs, in the order the office's manuals set out: Examination, Investigation, Decision Findings, Decision, Implementation, Representation, Disposal, Documents and Note Sheet. Following the manuals meant officers didn't have to relearn their process, only where it lived.

  • 01Verify before admittingCNIC is checked first, so every admitted case is tied to a real person.
  • 02Drafts build on each otherFindings feed the decision, so nothing is retyped.
  • 03Reach people how they listenWhatsApp, SMS, email or post, because citizens use different channels.
H.05

Run by the office, not by developers

Roles, permissions, complaint categories and regional offices are managed in the admin panel, so when a department changes or a new office opens, staff update it themselves. Categories and offices are switched off, not deleted, so old cases keep their history.

05 Iteration

What staff changed.

Staff reviewed shared Figma prototypes at each stage. Their feedback went straight into the next version before handoff.

I.01

Tabs instead of sub-menus

The first design nested each module's pages as sub-items in the side menu, which made the menu long and meant a lot of scrolling. Those pages became tabs inside the module, so the menu stays short and the screen space goes to the work.

I.02

Multiple documents in one upload

The first design took one file at a time. A complaint often comes with several supporting papers and required documents, so the uploader now takes them together.

What changed for each group

  • 01CitizensFile from a phone instead of visiting an office, and see every step of their case. 18,000+ complaints came in during the first 9 months, against 7–8K a year on paper.
  • 02StaffFind any case by CNIC or reference number instead of searching registers.
  • 03OfficersSee pending, overdue and escalated cases live. Average resolution fell from 120 to 50 days.

What the design did, and didn't, cause

These are system-level results. Moving off paper, the 15+ city rollout and the office's own work all contributed. The design's part was making filing short enough to do on a phone, and making every case findable and trackable.

07 Learnings

What I'd take to the next project.

L.01

Urdu is a layout, not a translation

Planning right to left from the wireframes kept Urdu from becoming a mirrored copy of English with broken spacing. Next time I'd review the Urdu screens with staff in the same rounds as English, not after.

L.02

Agree on the baseline in week one

The paper process kept almost no data, so the before numbers had to be estimated from manuals and registers. On the next project I'd agree with the client on what to measure before launch.

L.03

Research through the people closest to the work

With no access to citizens, staff were our best source on where complaints got stuck. Remote sessions worked, but next time I'd push to observe the counter in person at least once.
