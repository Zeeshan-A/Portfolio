NATJAN: a vendor portal redesigned, without starting over.

Vendors for a US facilities company ran their work through a portal that had every feature but no guidance. The clearest case was a payment decision hidden in small print. As the only designer, I redesigned the vendor portal on a themed version of its existing MUI library, without a rebuild, and designed the inspection and checklist flows for a new mobile app.

01 Context

What is NATJAN?

NATJAN is the janitorial management system of The Facilities Group (TFG), a US corporate facilities company. It has three products: a vendor portal, an internal portal for TFG staff, and a mobile app for people on site.

The vendor portal already worked, and developers had built it on a stock MUI component library. The brief was to make it clearer without throwing that work away, and to design the new mobile app's core flows from scratch.

RoleWhereWhat they do
VendorsWeb portal and mobile appReceive work orders, log crew visits, upload documents, invoice, and choose how fast they get paid
Area ManagersMobile appInspect sites in their area, including unplanned visits
Regional ManagersMobile appInspect across their region, with the same flow as Area Managers

My part

I was the only designer, working with a PM, a scrum master, the client's product owner and the developers. The PM and scrum master owned the requirements. I audited the existing portal, led the technical study with the developers that led to theming MUI instead of rebuilding, proposed the new information architecture to the client, and redesigned the vendor portal across 5 breakpoints. On mobile, I designed the inspection, vendor and checklist flows. The client reviewed and approved each module before handoff.

02 MUI Customization

Theme MUI, don't replace it.

After a technical study with the developers, we kept MUI and changed how it looks: a new theme, tokens and restyled components. Working features kept running, developers kept the APIs they knew, and one theme covered 300+ screens. I customized the library, applied it to the main screens to show the direction, and the client approved it.

  • TypographyRobotoInstrument Sans
  • Corner radiusSharp 4px cornersSofter 8px on cards, buttons, dialogs and inputs
  • ColourStock MUI paletteNATJAN's primary colour as the main theme
  • Shadows and bordersDefault MUI elevationSubtler, softer shadows and lighter borders
  • Buttons and inputsDefault MUI stylingSame sizing, with the new radius and primary colour
  • IconsMaterial IconsLucide, an open-source icon library

03 Problem

The portal worked. It just didn't guide anyone.

Vendors had every tool they needed, but the portal gave them no sense of what mattered or what to do next. The one screen with real money at stake, QuickPay, hid its options behind legal text, and the navigation was cluttered. On site, managers had no way to record an unplanned inspection. The redesign had to fix this without a rebuild, because the developers' working code had to stay.

QuickPay was the clearest case, but not the only one. The portal also had a cluttered navigation, lists with no KPI summary cards, and an unclear visual hierarchy across its screens. The problems below are the ones with the most evidence behind them.

02 A

QuickPay: a money decision buried in small print

Automatic QuickPay lets a vendor trade a fee for faster payment on every invoice. It's a real financial commitment, yet the old screen asked vendors to make it without showing the options or what they would cost. The client heard this from vendors in its regular meetings with them and passed the issues to us.

  1. 1
    The rates were hidden

    All five payment terms sat inside one dropdown, so vendors couldn't compare them before choosing.

  2. 2
    Consent came before the choice

    A paragraph of small italic legal text and a checkbox had to be dealt with first.

  3. 3
    A button that stayed off

    Opt-in stayed disabled until both were done, with nothing saying why.

02 B

A cluttered navigation

Single items opened a sub-menu for no reason, labels were long ("List of work orders"), and secondary items sat among the main tasks.

02 C

No way to record an ad-hoc inspection

Area and Regional Managers visit sites without a planned work order, but there was no mobile flow to record those inspections. This came up in client meetings.

02 D

Work orders with no overview

The old Work Orders list opened at 1,000 rows per page, with grid tools (Columns, Density, Find unique values) above the data and no summary of what was open, due or overdue. Every column had the same weight, so nothing told a vendor where to look first.

04 Investigation

Three sources, one list of fixes.

I didn't have direct access to vendors, so the evidence came from three places.

05 Design decisions

Same portal, clearer at every step.

D.01

A full UI overhaul, built for data

Answers 02 B and 02 D

The portal kept its features, but every screen was rebuilt around one idea: the data is the work, so the frame around it should stay quiet. Drag the line to compare the old Work Orders list with the new one.

  • 01A dark frame, a light workspaceThe navigation sits on a dark navy panel, and the data-heavy body stays white and light grey. The contrast splits the screen into two clear zones, so vendors focus on one area at a time.
  • 02KPIs before rowsFour cards for open work orders, due this week with the overdue count, completed in 30 days and total NTE value come before the table, so the first thing a vendor sees is what needs attention.
  • 03A simpler navigationTop-level items open directly, labels are short, and secondary items moved to the bottom of the panel.
  • 04A cleaner, calmer UIOne toolbar instead of stacked grid tools, colour tags for categories, 12 rows per page instead of 1,000, and Lucide icons in place of stock Material Icons.
D.02

QuickPay: choose, then confirm

Answers 02 A

All five payment terms are now visible as options, so vendors can compare them before choosing. The legal text became three plain-language points after the choice, not before it. A confirmation dialog shows the term, the fee and a worked example on a $1,000 invoice before anything changes. By design, opting in now takes 3 clicks instead of 4: pick a term, Opt-in, then Agree and opt-in.

AspectBeforeAfter
Payment termsFive rates hidden in one dropdownAll five visible as options, one preselected
ConsentItalic legal paragraph and a checkbox, before anything elseThree plain-language points, after the choice
CostA percentage onlyA worked example on a $1,000 invoice
WarningsP.O. box rule buried in the legal textIts own highlighted warning
Clicks to opt in4: tick, open dropdown, pick, Opt-in3: pick a term, Opt-in, Agree and opt-in
D.03

A shorter, flatter navigation

Answers 02 B

Single items no longer open a sub-menu, labels are short ("Work orders", not "List of work orders"), and secondary items moved to the bottom of the panel. I proposed the structure in client meetings, and we refined it together in Figma before any visual design.

AspectBeforeAfter
Sub-itemsSingle items opened a sub-menu for no reasonItems open directly; sub-menus only where there are real sub-pages
LabelsLong labels, like "List of work orders"Short labels, like "Work orders"
Secondary itemsMixed in with the main tasksMoved to the bottom of the panel
D.04

One work order, one page

A work order's information, recurrence, IVR, crew, notes and documents each have a tab, so vendors work from one page instead of moving between screens.

D.05

Five breakpoints, one overview

The client's Google Analytics showed vendors using the portal on a wide range of screen sizes, so the overview was designed for 5 breakpoints. On tablets the sidebar collapses to labelled icons; on phones it moves into a menu. Summary cards stack two by two, and action buttons shrink to icons so the list stays in view.

D.06

Mobile: inspections for Area and Regional Managers

Answers 02 C

Managers had no way to record an unplanned inspection. The flow finds a nearby site, checks them in within 100 m of the store so the visit is verified, runs the checklist and checks out with a summary. Area and Regional Managers share the same flow and switch roles from the side panel.

D.07

Mobile: a guided vendor visit

The app recommends the next work order, verifies the vendor is at the store, then walks them through before and after photos, notes, a checklist and an e-signature. Together these are TFG's proof of work, so check-out unlocks only when everything is done.

D.08

Checklists: one question at a time

Instead of one long form, each question gets its own screen: single choice, multiple choice, a rating, a reading, a date or photos. One question at a time is easier to read and answer, a step bar shows progress, and there's a review before submitting.

06 Iteration

Module by module, through client review.

Every module went through the same loop, tracked in Jira. Each design task was linked to its change request or bug, so it was always clear what was waiting on whom.

I.01

The review loop

  1. 01
    Analyze the existing portal

    Find usability issues, inconsistencies and pain points. Each design task is linked to its change request or bug.

  2. 02
    Plan the improvements

    Redesign screens with new UI patterns, consistent with the design system and responsive across screen sizes.

  3. 03
    Client review

    Present, gather feedback and revise. The task waits on the client until the screens are approved.

  4. 04
    Handoff

    Organized Figma files, specs and assets for the developers, then the task closes.

New features went through a longer path: requirements, low-fidelity wireframes, client approval, high-fidelity design, a second approval and handoff.

I.02

One pin colour became a map key

The managers' map view first used a single pin colour for every site. In review, it was clear that managers needed to tell work orders from inspections at a glance. Pins are now colour-coded by type, with a legend on the map.

07 Outcomes

A familiar portal, now built to guide.

The redesign is shipped. It changed what vendors see on every screen without changing the code underneath.

08 Learnings

What I'd take to the next project.

L.01

Customizing can beat rebuilding

Theming MUI changed the experience without throwing away working code. Agreeing that with the developers before designing kept every screen buildable.

L.02

Agree on a number before launch

QuickPay was a clear UX problem, and the redesign solved it. Next time I'd agree one number with the client before launch, such as the opt-in rate, so the improvement can be shown, not just seen.
